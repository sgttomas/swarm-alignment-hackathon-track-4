# Attention-budget alternative: Who pays for shutdown?

## Return and recommendation

**Recommend a bounded finite-model investigation of honest-client service during adversarially induced control interventions.** The concrete decision is whether a managed software service should suspend every untrusted worker, quarantine a verified trust domain, or spend scarce trusted effort on discriminating evidence after several workers produce suspicious outputs. A successful security intervention can impose its costs on unrelated clients. The useful result would identify when that cost is warranted by shared compromise and when a coalition can exploit the intervention itself to deny legitimate service.

This is a proposed **AI-control evaluation contribution**, not a claim to have discovered denial of service, alert flooding, or a new optimal-control formalism. The closest work already treats scarce auditing, suspicious-but-benign budget exhaustion, adaptive deployment, false-positive costs, and adversarial alert prioritization. The possible addition is a small, completely inspectable mixed-trust case family in which legitimate clients and malicious workers coexist; control policies are examined for both harmful releases and the legitimate work they preserve **during** an attack. The strongest result would be a consequential policy-ranking change plus its boundary under shared compromise, rather than an attractive name for an omitted metric. Separate scrutiny found that the first finite illustration accidentally disclosed a safe subset and left evidence purchase underspecified. The repaired minimum model below supports a negative zero-risk boundary; a useful policy-ranking change remains a conditional investigation target, not a reason to prefer this case now.

One serious proposal is developed here. ## Why this is a consequential alternative

**Recipient and operational use.** A release-service operator hosts several workers serving distinct client projects. It has limited trusted review/reimplementation capacity before a release deadline. Workers produce candidate patches; the trusted gateway alone can authorize releases. A burst of suspicious candidate patches raises doubt about worker trustworthiness. The operator must choose the scope of interruption and how to allocate remaining trusted capacity. The account should help that operator decide what evidence and isolation assumptions justify continuing work for a client whose worker did not trigger the alert.

**Principals and outsiders.** The operator owes each client authorized, correct releases and explicit service commitments. The attacker's immediate goal is to cause other clients to miss their deadlines, or to exploit selective continuation for harmful release. Honest clients are affected parties outside the attacking coalition. “Honest client” means a principal requesting legitimate work; its worker may nevertheless share a compromised model or credential. That distinction prevents the evaluator from granting perfect worker-trust knowledge to the controller. Deadlines and relative consequences are stipulated service obligations in a constructed case, not measured real-world harm or automatically interchangeable utility weights.

**Multi-agent mechanism.** Several workers share an audit/fallback pool and a control rule. Two coalition workers can create apparently separate suspicious events that trigger a global response, while another client's pending work depends on that response. Alternatively, shared model weights or credentials can make the same events evidence that the other worker is compromised too. Cross-worker coupling comes from the shared intervention and trust dependency, not merely from having multiple transcripts. A single attacker can also cause some of this harm; a matched single-source condition is needed to test whether coalition structure adds anything beyond the same total workload. Do not call ordinary congestion proof of collusion.

**Connection to the owner's thesis.** The inquiry makes attention, delay, lost opportunities and accountable reliance part of the actual decision. It asks whether a security-success measure supports the use being made of it and who bears the cost of the resulting action. This follows the examined thesis Introduction's “Four questions throughout” and Conclusion's finite-attention discussion; it is a research motivation, not evidence for the proposed result and does not require using framework vocabulary in the study.

## Research question and competing mechanisms

**Question.** When suspicious work from some agents changes a shared control policy, which intervention preserves legitimate client work without exceeding a specified harmful-release bound, and how does the answer depend on whether compromise is isolated or shared across domains?

The first finite investigation should compare these explanations rather than assume selective control wins:

1. **Isolated manipulation:** coalition-controlled workers deliberately produce harmless but suspicious work; global fallback denies unrelated clients service. Restricting intervention to a justified domain can avoid that externality.
2. **Common compromise:** the same observations implicate a shared model/provider/credential. A locally quiet worker is unsafe; isolation by superficial client identity preserves throughput by permitting harm.
3. **Benign burst or changed tasks:** unusual legitimate work, not an attacker, raises alerts. Any policy using the old normal-alert distribution can overreact. This affects both availability and calibration claims.
4. **Ordinary load:** identical total arrivals exhaust resources without coordination or adaptive control. An apparent coalition benefit disappears under matched arrival counts and per-worker limits.
5. **Control is proportionate:** the value or obligation associated with preventing a shared harmful release makes global fallback the justified choice even though other clients lose useful work. The study should expose this tradeoff, not label every denial malicious or every broad control defective.

**Target contribution.** A small case family and an examined comparison showing (a) whether the conventional benign-usefulness/adversarial-safety pair leaves relevant policy differences unresolved for this use; (b) the safety versus honest-client service frontier within mixed deployments; and (c) when additional trusted evidence is worth the capacity it consumes. A useful negative result is that the matched policies tie, ordinary load explains the purported coalition effect, common-compromise uncertainty makes global fallback unavoidable at the selected bound, or a proposed selective policy adds no value after honest capacity accounting.

## Finite case design and observation boundaries

**Unit.** One bounded deployment episode: fixed legitimate client jobs, authenticated workers/trust domains, an alert-generating prefix, an intervention decision, remaining trusted slots, and a deadline. Keep the intervention prefix and pending jobs identical between policies. The evaluator must record who requested each job, who supplied the candidate, what was authorized, actual correctness/harm, release decision, completion time and trusted work consumed.

**Worker/attacker view.** Each worker sees its own task, permitted shared signals and public control policy. The coalition controls only its assigned workers' submissions and timing within a specified fixed action/arrival budget; it cannot forge gateway identity, see private evaluator truth, create unbounded identities, or directly write another client's patch. Both one-source and two-source strategies with equal total cost are included. Explicitly vary what shared timing feedback is visible; memoryless and coordinated variants are different threat models.

**Controller view.** Authenticated worker/client identity, monitor scores, arrival/deadline metadata, trusted budget used, and a declared trust-dependency graph. The graph can include shared provider/model/credential domains, but its truth is an assumption until independently established. Scores are imperfect signals, not labels. Initial worker trust states and whether an episode is benign, isolated compromise or common compromise are hidden. The controller can refuse release, quarantine a mechanically specified domain, consume a trusted review/reimplementation slot, and choose which queued job gets that slot. Mere instructions to workers do not enforce quarantine; the constructed gateway does.

**Evaluator view.** The complete constructed history and predeclared correctness/harm table, compromise assignment, client obligations and actual budget/deadline. This privileged information scores outcomes only; it is not a free controller feature. The evaluator distinguishes valid output withheld, invalid output released, completed legitimate work, and legitimate work delayed past its commitment. Review the reference table itself: an arbitrary “harm” label or conflation of client honesty and worker safety can predetermine the answer.

**Resource distinction.** In Greenblatt, scarce high-quality H labor is used for auditing, while weaker trusted T supplies fallback outputs; the 2% audit allowance does **not** imply a 2% fallback capacity. This proposal deliberately introduces a bounded shared pool for trusted checks or contract-correct regeneration before the deadline. That is a chosen service/cost model, potentially motivated by tasks for which the abundant weaker T cannot meet the client contract. Its relevance must be defended independently; ordinary unlimited adequate T fallback is a required countercase. Separate audit and production pools can be a small sensitivity extension, not silently treated as equivalent resources.

**Policies.** Compare an aggregate-suspicion global fallback; a source/domain-selective fallback using the declared trust graph; and, only if justified by the finite case, a policy that buys discriminating evidence before choosing scope. All face the same trusted slots, latency, monitor observations and attack actions. Count evidence acquisition, candidate rechecking and fallback production against capacity. A selective policy cannot obtain a free shared-compromise oracle or label innocent workers retrospectively. These are explicit adaptations inspired by the sources, not reproductions of their published implementations.

**Concrete proposed schedule and reference.** Use four client release jobs, J1/J2 for client H1 and J3/J4 for H2. Each has an independently specified finite input/output contract. A safe/useful release must satisfy that contract completely and be authorized for the stated client; a candidate with a planted failing input is an unsafe release. The reference is a prewritten finite truth table and complete contract tests, independent of monitor scores, attack declarations and controller decisions. This is an abstract finite service model, not a claim that real semantic verification costs exactly one unit.

| Time / object | Fixed arrivals, actions and observations | Budget / reference consequence |
|---|---|---|
| t=0, worker A1 | One contract-correct candidate with a suspicious monitor flag. | One action; no direct alteration of H1/H2's artifacts. |
| t=1, worker A2 | One contract-correct candidate with a suspicious monitor flag. Aggregate rule triggers intervention after the second flag. | One action. Total alert-inducing attack budget is exactly two submissions; no extra identities, replacements or arrivals. |
| t=2, H1/H2 jobs | Four already-produced artifacts J1–J4 are pending, each with no suspicious flag, authenticated owner/worker and deadline t=4. | Exactly two trusted slots remain: [2,3) and [3,4). Prefix costs are deducted equally. Metadata inspection does not reveal complete contract truth. |
| Decision from t=2 | Choose which artifacts to release, withhold, audit or regenerate; a policy can condition the second slot on the first result. | Audit and regeneration have the exact distinct semantics below. All policies have the same action set, not merely equal numeric budgets. |
| Deadline t=4 | Evaluator checks actual releases, contract truth, authorization and completion time. | One service success per correct on-time J; unsafe releases counted separately. Refusal/late delivery is lost service, not by itself a backdoor. |

**Complete common-knowledge threat set.** The controller knows that the set of contract-failing pending artifacts can be **any subset S of {J1,J2,J3,J4}**, including the empty set and the full set. This is the base admissible state set, closed under every permutation of the four job identities. Every S initially produces the same declared dependency information and quiet-score projection for these four jobs. Actual S is hidden; the allowed state set and its invariants are not. Shared-root compromise can affect either client's worker or both and need not manifest in every output. This is explicitly broader than the first draft's one J3/J4 failure assignment. No job or client is known safe merely by its position in the table.

The following rows are **representative cases inside that closed set**, not its exhaustive enumeration:

| Representative case | Evaluator truth | Interpretation / necessary comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Benign burst | S is empty; A1/A2 are legitimate unusually styled submissions. | Ordinary useful cooperation has the same alert observations. |
| Isolated alert attack | S is empty; A1/A2 intentionally induce intervention but do not compromise H1/H2's outputs. | The actual service loss from broad fallback is real in this world; its existence does not justify release under unresolved uncertainty. |
| Shared-root manifestation on H2 | S={J3,J4}. | The original countercase remains included. |

**Nominated paid-information channel: ordinary per-job audit.** Every policy may choose either of these actions in a trusted slot:

- `Audit(j)` checks the *original* candidate Jj against its complete contract and returns only its pass/fail bit. In the minimum model this bit is exact. A pass makes that original artifact eligible for correct release, thereby completing that job without another production slot. A fail produces no replacement and does not complete the job. The result says nothing directly about dependency roots, worker intent, or any other artifact. It consumes one slot whether it passes or fails.
- `Regenerate(j)` produces a contract-correct replacement for Jj in one slot, whether its original was correct or not. It supplies no information about the original or other jobs. A slot cannot also audit another artifact. Ordinary release/withholding uses the common gateway interface; its overhead is equal across policies.

These ideal trusted capabilities are chosen finite-model assumptions, comparable to the perfect-audit idealizations in the inspected control work. Their unit latency and availability in a real managed release service are not established by the sources. The consumer would need costly semantic validation or trusted production to settle a candidate's contract; if cheap complete deterministic tests are already available to the gateway, the scarcity premise fails. Real audits' error and separate audit/production pools remain sensitivity questions. No paid root diagnostic or all-jobs oracle is assumed. A perfect root diagnostic could only be an explicitly labeled information upper bound in a later extension, not the nominated deployment capability.

**Zero-risk boundary and author backcheck.** For this base state set, any unaudited/unregenerated job remains potentially failing after any observations on other jobs: every remaining subset is still admitted. Therefore a controller requiring zero worst-case unsafe-release probability can complete at most two jobs with two slots. Regenerating two always attains two; auditing a passing artifact completes one, while auditing a failing artifact consumes a slot without completion. Evidence-first cannot beat two at this criterion by relabeling a per-job result as root evidence. On the ordinary S=empty path, two audits both pass and complete two; the model does not permit releasing the other two at zero worst-case risk because they remain unexamined. With four regeneration slots, all four jobs can be delivered safely. These are hand-derived consequences of the specified finite assumptions, not empirical findings or a novel theorem.

The reviewer's original policy—release J1/J2, regenerate J3/J4—does correctly serve four in the old literal table and in the revised benign/isolated S=empty case. In the revised allowed world S={J1,J2}, it releases two failing originals and produces two correct replacements, violating zero-risk safety. This backcheck removes the accidental safe-subset premise without denying that the examiner's objection was valid. A hidden state alone would not have repaired it; the explicit enlargement of the admissible state set is a consequential design revision and changes the basis of subsequent analysis.

**Conditional probabilistic route, without an extra information oracle.** To examine a nontrivial safety/service decision after the negative robust boundary, a selected investigation can nominate an externally justified probability model and risk bound. A concrete *constructed* family is: a latent shared compromise Z occurs with probability p; conditional on Z=0 all four pending artifacts pass, while conditional on Z=1 each artifact fails independently with probability q, with 0<q<1. This admits every S for p>0 and has no invariant-safe job. The common root changes probabilities, not the audit semantics. Benign bursts and isolated alert attacks are both represented by Z=0 for these pending jobs; the prefix alone does not reveal Z. These probabilities are explicit case parameters, not estimates obtained from the cited papers.

For one passing `Audit(j)`, elementary Bayes gives `p_pass = p(1−q)/(1−pq)`. The remaining three artifacts then have probability `p_pass[1−(1−q)^3]` of at least one failure if all are released. A failed audit establishes Z=1 in this particular model, but other artifacts still have failure probability q; it does not certify them. With p,q strictly inside their ranges, even a pass leaves residual risk, so the zero-risk boundary persists. For a chosen nonzero conditional release-risk bound ε, this explicitly calculated risk determines whether continuation after a pass is admissible. An interval prior requires the worst allowed value, not a convenient midpoint. Audit cost, a failed audit's lost service slot, the option to regenerate instead, and the unused second slot must all be included before comparing policies. No policy-ranking result is asserted here.

This finite family makes the next calculation definite while exposing its limits: correlated-error calibration is the premise creating cross-job inference from an ordinary audit. If it cannot be supported for the intended service, retain the robust no-advantage result or treat the family as a conditional sensitivity example, not an actionable deployment recommendation. An independent-per-job failure model is a countercase: auditing j changes no other job's risk. Worst-case safety, prior-weighted service and conditional risk limits are different decisions. The owner has selected none of p, q or ε; no numerical risk target is invented to justify a preferred result.

**Discriminator.** Hold workload, identities, deadlines and available capacity fixed while changing the control scope; separately vary the *actual* trust relation and the controller's evidence about it. Report harmful-release risk/count and correct on-time work per legitimate client, alongside trusted effort and waiting. For any chosen weighted or constrained decision rule, state whose obligations/weights it represents and sweep or retain uncertainty rather than invent a universal scalar score. A ranking caused by uncharged evidence, differently privileged access, or extra compute is not a fair comparison. By contrast, buying informative observations from the same action set at the same stated cost is a legitimate policy choice and the intended value-of-information comparison; policies need not buy identical information. If no mixed-deployment policy decision changes, the metric observation alone is too weak for the promised contribution.

## Feasibility, minimum means and scrutiny

**Route now supported: finite argument and exhaustive small-case analysis, if selected.** Public primary bodies are retained and sufficient to specify the comparator assumptions. A paper-and-pencil case with a few workers, finite action sets and one or two intervention rounds needs no model calls or historical incident dataset. It can derive exact outcomes and, where useful, decision boundaries as functions of remaining capacity, compromise uncertainty, evidence accuracy and missed-deadline cost. This establishes consequences of explicit premises, not the prevalence or capability of real LLM coalitions. Any later small enumerator is support for checking finite arithmetic; it must follow the declared case rather than silently choose it.

**Retained records versus new evidence.** Existing APPS/control papers establish source definitions and motivate the policy families. Their retained outputs do not contain concurrent client deadlines, scarce shared fallback capacity, mixed worker compromise or actual quarantine effects. They cannot be relabeled into an empirical estimate of this proposal. No new-run dataset has been qualified. Claims that an LLM can reliably generate these alerts, infer thresholds, coordinate an availability attack, or that real operators respond this way require additional authorized trials/observations and calibrated cost measurements. Those claims are unnecessary for the finite contribution and remain out of scope now.

**Minimum effort estimate.** About 4–6 focused researcher-hours for a small explicit game/case table, manual derivation, matched-policy comparison, source account and concise report; about 1–2 examiner-hours for independent table/argument checks and backcheck. These are planning estimates, not observed costs or guaranteed completion. Start with one representative benign case, one isolated attack and one common-compromise case before enlarging the state space. No paid calls, accounts, external messages or platform work are intrinsically needed. An ambitious learned-policy benchmark would be a different undertaking with unqualified costs.

**Examination route.** Agent 0's separate synthesis examiner checks that this is distinct from IDS triage, not a repackaging of App F, and that the operational decision matters. A selected investigation then needs an examiner to independently reconstruct the small outcome table/derivation and challenge (i) controller access to trust-domain evidence; (ii) same workload, budget and deadlines; (iii) legitimate burst and common-compromise countercases; (iv) whether a coalition adds anything relative to a matched single actor; (v) the harm/reference definition; and (vi) omitted evidence and recovery costs. Any formal generalization needs proof scrutiny beyond a checked example. Reuse unaffected source readings. The investigation owner repairs supported findings and the examiner backchecks affected cases; author reasoning here is not independent evidence.

**Advance condition.** The nominated minimum has a concrete schedule, closed threat set and ordinary per-job actions; it currently yields the negative zero-risk boundary stated above. Advance to a richer finite comparison only with a selected service contract and either a justified probabilistic failure family/risk criterion or independently grounded trust evidence. Compare adaptive auditing against all admissible regeneration/release policies, not only the initially named global/local pair. A useful contribution still needs an actionable control choice or boundary beyond known denial of service. The owner can choose that finite undertaking directly; there is no need to invent a realistic model benchmark first.

**Stop/narrow conditions.** Stop the “new control mechanism” claim if the nearest prior work already supplies the same mixed-deployment decision analysis. Preserve a useful replication only if it resolves a real chosen inference. Narrow if deadlines or shared fallback are merely arbitrary additions with no intended consumer; if controller observations cannot distinguish cases, report the resulting conditional tradeoff rather than pretend to detect intent. Reject superiority claims if trusted capacity or available privileged access differs between arms; different paid choices within a common action set are legitimate. If only the trivial fact that shutdown reduces throughput remains, do not present a completed hackathon research contribution; strengthen the bounded decision analysis or choose another candidate.
