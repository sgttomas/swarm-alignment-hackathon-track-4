## Main finding

The three readings do **not** justify treating more cooperation, more agreement, greater coalition reward, or stronger commitment as an oversight improvement. They separate three questions: whether interaction produces desirable outcomes for a specified constituency; whether each actor has reason to follow the proposed arrangement; and what actors can actually observe and enforce. A mechanism can answer the second and third questions successfully while harming someone excluded from the first.

For the project's candidate E, reward sharing is a plausible experimental variable, but it is neither a sufficient definition of collusion nor the central oversight objective. The prior basis does not yet identify a complete relationship among the agents' private rewards, the principal's task outcome, the verifier's obligation, and affected outsiders. Its clean factorial comparison could estimate an interaction while leaving that more consequential question unresolved. Recovery may improve truthful verification, make coordinated deception more effective, or leave incentives unchanged. Which possibility matters depends on who receives evidence, who controls actions, and whose losses the verdict can cause.

## Source-grounded distinctions

**Cooperation is relational; it is not an automatic social good.** Conitzer and Oesterheld explicitly note undesirable cooperation, including pricing collusion and compromise of competitive AI safety arrangements (p.15359, footnote 1). Their Traveler's Dilemma example distinguishes humanity's objective, minimum service quality, from the agents' almost-aligned utilities; humanity is explicitly not a player (p.15360). Thus the analyst must include a principal or affected public even when that party is absent from the game. Their paper is a research agenda for advanced AI game theory, not evidence that current LLMs implement its proposed reasoning.

**Social welfare requires both a constituency and an aggregation rule.** Ivanov, Zisman and Chernyshev define the mediator's reward as the sum of rewards of agents in its coalition (§2.3); individual agents retain their own reward functions. A total can rise while one participant loses. Their Prisoner's Dilemma with Sacrifice (§4.3, Tables 5–8) makes this concrete: the total-maximizing outcome `(5,0)` sacrifices player 2, which can instead secure 1 through defection. A naive mediator cannot induce that player's commitment. A mixture of mutual cooperation `(2,2)` and sacrifice can respect the outside option while increasing the total. This is a participation constraint, not an egalitarian guarantee, and says nothing directly about unmodeled parties.

**Equilibrium, optimality, learning, and consent are distinct.** The mediation paper calls cooperation finding a socially beneficial equilibrium, emphasizing absence of profitable individual deviation (§1). Conitzer and Oesterheld emphasize that many equilibria can exist, standard learning can reach a poor one, and independently choosing incompatible favorable equilibria need not produce an equilibrium at all (pp.15364–15365). Existence of a good equilibrium therefore does not establish discovery, selection, stability under a different deviation class, or acceptability to the principal. The mediation paper leaves robustness to deviations by *groups* to future work (§2.2). Its sentence that emergent behavior is “by design an equilibrium” (p.2) should not be imported as a general convergence theorem: the actual training is approximate, constraints use estimated counterfactual values, and finite learned policies can retain deviations or incomplete commitment (§§3–4).

**Commitment changes the game; recommendations need not.** In Mediated MARL, agents choose whether to cede action choice for a window of `k` steps; they cannot secretly act independently while reporting commitment (§§2.2–2.3). Centralized execution enforces this, and action-recommending mediators are explicitly future work (§5). Increasing the window can improve cumulative outcomes by allowing temporary sacrifices, but it changes when agents may reconsider (§3.2; Appendix A.1's two-step asymmetric game). A prompt saying “commit,” or a log saying “accepted,” supplies neither this execution authority nor these incentive properties.

**Verification has strategic effects.** Conitzer and Oesterheld's disarmament discussion requires verifiable reductions of available strategies before the other party proceeds (p.15361). Their program-game discussion shows that source transparency can enable cooperative equilibria; exact-copy cooperation is fragile, and the program-game analysis concerns rational designers choosing programs, not a solved account of how deployed learners reason (p.15362). Therefore reliable observations can change strategic possibilities as well as help an external auditor discover facts. No claim that source inspection alone predicts neural-agent behavior follows.

**Credible threats can induce agreement without restoring the no-threat outcome.** Landis and Schwartzbach study agents that commit to contracts conditional on other contracts, creating a metagame above the auction (§2). Their simple `n=3,m=2` example makes low-price lottery participation attractive relative to the alternative induced by the commitment. In §4, p.6, the low-valued agent's leadership can hurt the high-valued agent relative to the ordinary auction even though compliance is preferable to the threatened alternative. The text explicitly says “agent 3 is hurt by the attack.” This qualifies the abstract/§3 heading's broad “all buyers benefit” language. An opt-in arrangement is not automatically a Pareto improvement over the original situation; its outside option may itself have been worsened by the mechanism.

## What the three bodies actually establish, and their limits

### Foundations of Cooperative AI

The publisher title is **Foundations of Cooperative AI**, not “General Foundations of Cooperative AI.” Vincent Conitzer and Caspar Oesterheld; AAAI 37(13), 15359–15367 (2023), DOI `10.1609/aaai.v37i13.26791`. Read substantive body pp.15359–15365, including footnotes and algorithms; bibliography inspected for source identity only. There is no appendix in these bytes.

The agenda connects repeated games, disarmament, cooperation among copies, source-code transparency, self-locating beliefs, and equilibrium selection. Repeated play can support cooperation, conditional on discounting and other assumptions; equilibrium multiplicity and brittle transitions limit this explanation (pp.15360–15361). The copy/self-location sections distinguish causal from evidential decision theories and external designer from internal agent perspectives (pp.15361–15364); these remain open theoretical/implementation questions. I have not independently established the cited folk theorems, complexity results, decision-theoretic claims, or empirical studies. For this project the warranted contribution is a map of premises that alter interaction, particularly the distinction between individual alignment and collective outcomes and the dual use of transparency. It does not select a recovery mechanism or a reward experiment for us.

### Mediated Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning

Dmitry Ivanov, Ilya Zisman and Kirill Chernyshev; examined **arXiv:2306.08419v1**, submitted 2023-06-14. The PDF calls itself a slightly updated version of the AAMAS 2023 publication and asks readers to cite the publication. Read §§1–5 and Appendices A–B, including technical details and hyperparameters, in the 13-page PDF. This is the arXiv body, not a verified byte-identical final publisher edition.

The game has separate agents, observations, actions, transition dynamics and rewards. The mediator acts only for the coalition that commits. Its execution policy uses the individual actor observation plus coalition/index; its critic has centralized access to all agents' observations and rewards **during training** (§3.1). This distinction matters: an evaluator's retrospective global information cannot silently be handed to a deployed verifier.

The naive mediator optimizes coalition reward. The constrained version adds two conditions (§3.2, Eqs.6–7): a joining agent should do at least as well as if outside, and an outsider should do no better than if it joined. These conditions average over the coalition distribution, rather than requiring every coalition separately. Counterfactual values come from the learned critic. Lagrange multipliers are approximated as scalars; the implementation deliberately trades fidelity for simplicity. The resulting objective includes negative weight on outsiders' rewards to deter free riding (Eq.13 and following discussion). “Benevolent mediator” therefore means benevolent under a specified coalition/constraint objective; it is not an independently justified public-interest property.

The evidence is controlled RL in deliberately simple games where the efficient policy is known. The authors explain that this isolates incentive compatibility and consequently omit broader algorithm baselines (§4). Results include naive mediation working in the two-player PD, failing to prevent outsider free riding in public-good games, constrained mediation improving commitment, and longer windows helping in sequential settings. Appendix B specifies 50 seeds for the PD variants and 10 for public-good variants; the reported point summaries do not establish uncertainty for a new LLM peer-verification task. Larger-scale environments and action-recommending mediators are future work. No training or reproduction was performed here.

The strongest relevant comparator supplied by this paper is **naive aggregate-objective oversight versus individually incentive-compatible oversight under the same game and actual outside options**, not simply shared versus unshared scalar rewards. An oversight monitor may also require protection against coalitional deviations that this paper explicitly does not solve.

### Stackelberg Attacks on Auctions and Blockchain Transaction Fee Mechanisms

Daji Landis and Nikolaj I. Schwartzbach; examined **arXiv:2305.02178v1**, submitted 2023-05-03; 8 PDF pages. Read §§1–5 including Theorems 5/7 and their proof/proof sketch, §3's welfare derivation and concentration argument, §4's distributional proofs, and risk-aversion discussion. No appendix appears in these bytes. Reading these proofs is not full independent proof validation.

The model assumes one transaction/item demand per agent, distinct valuations, rational risk-neutral agents with quasilinear utility, public known valuations for analysis, uniform random tie-breaking, and enforceable contracts that can condition on other contracts (§2). The paper acknowledges exact valuation knowledge is false in practice and motivates approximate inference from concentration; that motivation is not a demonstrated deployment guarantee. The EIP-1559 model includes a burned base fee; “free” means negligible bids/tips in the relevant idealization, not universally zero total cost. §4's distributional analysis takes the base fee to be zero. Real contract execution, security, and adaptive blockchain behavior were not tested here or established empirically by this theoretical source.

The attack uses a privileged leader (or elite set) receiving slots with certainty, with others offered a low-price lottery, backed by a return to competitive bidding if they do not follow the contract. Its formal contribution is existence of changed equilibria under commitment and sufficient valuation conditions, not measurement of how often real agents collude. The source's “price of defiance” uses **bidders' net utility**, excluding auctioneer revenue (§3, Eq.5). Reduced payments can raise that sum while reducing seller revenue and changing allocative efficiency. Calling this a rise in general social welfare would lose the constituency boundary. Whether auctioneer revenue, buyer access, allocation quality, network viability or external costs should dominate is a separate normative/design decision.

Author checks found limitations that must accompany reliance on quantitative claims:

- Theorem 5's p.4 proof prints compliance when outside utility is **greater than** lottery utility. The prose and the resulting threshold require the reverse. I inspected the rendered PDF, so this is not extraction damage. Ignoring epsilon, with `p=(m-k)/(n-k)`, compliance requires `p(v-B) >= v-B-v_cut`, giving `(v-B)/v_cut <= (n-k)/(n-m)`. Thus correcting this local sign restores the claimed direction; it does not defeat the qualitative commitment example. The full theorem's contract/order/index conditions were not independently proved.
- Lemma 9, p.5, claims all uniform order statistics are within `O-tilde(1/n)` of their means with negligible failure probability. Its displayed exponent passes from a scale `delta^2 n/(1+4 delta)` to `Omega(delta n)`, then selects `delta=log^2(n)/(n+1)`. Direct substitution into the preceding expression instead has scale `log^4(n)/n`, which tends to zero and does not give the claimed negligible bound. Also its assertion `max(v,c0)=c0` is inconsistent with the displayed central-order scales `v=O(n^-1/2)` and `c0=O(n^-1)` (at equal Beta parameters actual `c0=0`). I visually checked p.5. This is a demonstrated gap in the displayed derivation; I have not supplied a repaired concentration argument or disproved Theorem 10 itself. Do not rely on the source's exact high-probability welfare bound as independently validated by this reading. The basic three-agent strategic comparison and outsider harm do not require Lemma 9.

These findings limit source reliance. They are not a proposal to turn an isolated source defect into the project's next research case. The numerical congestion thresholds in §4 remain source claims, not checked results of this contribution.

## Consequences for A–E and the next useful question

**A — label provenance:** the distinction among assigned target, observed coordination, agreement and harm becomes more important. A coalition may coordinate successfully without reducing the nominated principal's welfare, while strategically harmful outcomes can arise without the detector's expected communication pattern. An evaluator-provenance audit becomes useful when it identifies a consequential deployment decision distorted by the label, rather than merely finding an internally inconsistent helper. The three assigned readings do not resolve A's release-to-paper linkage or scientific consequence.

**B — public evidence:** missing communication does not rule out commitment, shared-code reasoning, equilibrium selection, or shared incentives. Conversely, a visible agreement does not show a deviation from the principal's objective. The archive limitations already identified remain; these sources do not create missing outcomes or authenticate messages. A graph-only forensic result cannot answer whose interests were served without additional economic/task context.

**C — completion and blame:** a correct state check remains valuable, but its meaning is objective-relative. Correct execution of an agent's strategy can still hurt the principal; correct task completion does not ensure a sound verifier verdict; an authorized party may reasonably reject participation despite higher coalition output. C needs the downstream decision and its costs specified before a fault label, preserving the existing wrong-reference alternatives.

**D — selective repair:** provenance correctness answers a real operational question but does not by itself make a correction socially legitimate or incentive-compatible. Different principals may disagree about the correction and whether previously committed behavior can change. These readings strengthen D's need to specify authority and actual outside options, but supply no new evidence that its proposed repair policy improves on the closest safeguards. D remains possible, not favored merely because its artifacts are easier to inspect.

**E — recovery in peer verification:** the premise is sound as a bounded question about one reward mechanism, but too narrow as the explanation of harmful coordination. Keep recovery availability separate from (i) the obligation to verify, (ii) the objective rewarded, (iii) the ability to deviate or to bind future behavior, and (iv) the principal's actual outcome. Specifying exact reward functions, already an open condition in E, should be part of *forming* its question, not postponed as routine trial setup. Without this, “shared incentives induce invalid agreement” remains a plausible narrative rather than a defined causal mechanism.

A stronger next case brief would answer: **When does a verifier have both the evidence and the incentive to protect its principal's task outcome, despite profitable cooperation with the actor being verified?** Potential recipients are an operator who delegates checks and a mechanism designer deciding how to reward/authorize verification. The desired failure is a principal-relevant wrong release/acceptance, distinct from mere procedural noncompliance or coalition agreement. Candidate explanations include unavailable evidence, a false reference, a reward for mutual acceptance, cost avoidance, credible retaliation/commitment, and coordinated deviations outside an individually robust scheme.

For E, the minimum comparator repair is to hold the principal's task objective and each agent's original utility interpretation explicit, then compare recovery with no recovery **within a specified incentive/authority structure**. Useful alternatives are a verifier rewarded for independently correct judgments, a peer rewarded for mutual acceptance, and an action-enforcing mediator versus an advisory check. These are candidate design distinctions, not selected arms: changing all of them at once would destroy interpretability. A nonstrategic or single-verifier condition may distinguish generic retrieval benefits from peer influence, but the closest task source and available resources must justify that addition. Coalition deviation and commitment robustness require different tests from unilateral best responses.

**Disposition:** withdraw this lane's support for E's present preference. Retain A–D as provisional alternatives, with their existing consequence/novelty gaps; do not promote them by default. These readings do not establish a current novel-study winner, and do not warrant selecting a new theoretical case merely because it is tractable. Harmful cooperation, strategic verification and coalition boundaries are established prior questions, not newly discovered contributions here.

**Strongest challenge to current selection:** E was preferred for an interaction estimand before establishing that its rewards, verdict rule and beneficiary constitute the intended oversight conflict. It can yield a clean interaction without teaching an operator which cooperation to enable, which to prevent, or whose welfare an intervention protects. **The next concrete bounded choice is a source-to-case contract examination of the actual peer-verification setting**, before dependent tooling or trials: identify the task principal and any separately affected parties; map the original rewards and consequences of each verdict; distinguish no-intervention, no-recovery and threatened outside options; specify who may receive raw evidence, including privacy/policy costs; and test whether valid task completion and valid verification can come apart in one ordinary and one harmful-cooperation case. Return either a revised, principal-relevant question with a meaningful comparator or a supported decision to drop E's preference. A per-agent incentive-compatible scheme cannot stand in for coalition-proof oversight, and separated rewards cannot be presumed a safe baseline. If no meaningful conflict survives, narrow to an evidence-recovery replication or reconsider the case; protocol audit feasibility alone is not scientific priority. The assigned sources support this correction, but cannot settle E's actual reward semantics or its nearest-prior-work novelty.
