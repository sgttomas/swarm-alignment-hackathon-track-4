## Source-first synthesis

### The population and its control problem

D's distinctive premise is the **patchwork AGI hypothesis**, not a finding that present swarms are AGI. Complementary skills, tools, scaffolds and task delegation may produce capabilities no constituent possesses. A collective may be an organized group agent with distinct representational/motivational states, or a system whose dispersed decisions aggregate through market mechanisms (§1 pp.1–3). Its unit is therefore not necessarily one fixed team under one orchestrator: capabilities may emerge across an economy, with changing membership, spawned copies, resource flows and even narrowly tasked humans (§2 pp.3–5). Communication/discoverability and economic adoption are consequential premises; low integration costs may accelerate growth, while transaction costs can delay it. Detection proposals include a collective task frontier, novel complementary collaborations and consolidation of interaction subgraphs (§2 p.5; §3.3.3 pp.13–14). These are proposed indicators, not validated AGI criteria. A dense graph or correlated success by itself is not demonstrated dangerous general capability.

I defines **agent infrastructure** as external technical systems/shared protocols that mediate an agent's interactions and environmental impacts (§1 pp.3–4). The agent includes its model and basic primitives/scaffolding; infrastructure is not simply its memory or compute. An **instance** corresponds to a user's interaction history, tools and memory, and need not have separate hardware (§2.1 pp.4–5, footnote 5). This gives a different population problem: heterogeneous agents and counterparties, inside and across organizations, need attribution, interaction rules and recourse. A user, developer, deployer, manager, service provider, insurer and harmed counterparty may have different information and authority. Binding one legal identity does not establish exclusive responsibility (§3.1 p.6), and infrastructure is “a platform for policies and norms,” not an allocation of liability (§8 p.21). The study deliberately leaves diffuse effects such as unemployment outside its main catalog (§2.1 p.5; §8).

A imagines many agents as faithful representatives holding separate private information, with differing objectives and interactions across digital and physical institutions (p.4). Its organising problem is reducing **Coasean transaction costs** while preserving secure, pluralistic coordination. The contract abstraction is broader than legal contracts: it includes security protocols (pp.8–9, footnote 1). Coordination means negotiation, verification and mobilisation despite competing goals, information asymmetry and adversarial environments (p.7). The required interfaces extend from fuzzy user preferences → formal security policy → negotiated collective policy → actual protocol → inspectable report (pp.10–12). Correct execution of a protocol is only one part: policy elicitation and negotiation can themselves lose the user's intent.

### What interventions assume and what they promise

D proposes four complementary layers: market design, baseline agent safety, monitoring/oversight and external regulatory mechanisms (§3, Table 1 p.7). The market layer includes gated I/O sandboxes, incentive/externality pricing, tamper-resistant activity provenance, circuit breakers, persistent IDs tied to legal owners, reputation/staking, contract verification, roles/access controls, anti-injection sanitation and collective capability restrictions. Baseline safety adds adversarial robustness, interruptibility, containment, alignment, interpretability and prompt defence. Monitoring must cover interaction/dependency/resource flows and forensic reconstruction, with independent human institutions able to intervene. Legal liability, standards, insurance, anti-monopoly measures, international coordination and capture-resistant governance sit outside the market's code (§3.4 pp.14–16).

The qualifications matter. Defence in depth depends on component failures being largely uncorrelated (§3 p.6). Every external channel threatens leakage, while complete isolation loses economic usefulness; human checks can be overwhelmed or manipulated (§3.1.1 p.6). Safety raises latency/compute costs and may cause adverse selection (§3.1.2 p.8). Semantic contract verification needs an oracle; the footnote explicitly requires safeguarding and independently assessing AI judges (§3.1.7 p.10 fn.1). Distributed interruption must track delegated subprocesses and include safe resumption (§3.2.2 p.11). Interpretability does not replace behavioural evaluation (§3.2.5 p.12). Static thresholds can be gamed (§3.3.1 p.13), and governance infrastructure can itself be captured or weaponised (§3.4.6 p.16). The conclusion says many measures are “yet to be fully developed in practice” (§4 p.16). A catalog of protections is not a demonstrated system-level safety argument.

I makes intervention interfaces more specific: identity binding; certification; agent IDs; agent-specific channels; oversight layers; communication protocols; commitment devices; incident reporting; and rollbacks (Table 1 p.3). An oversight layer is a **monitor plus an interface through which someone can intervene**, not merely a detector (§4.2 p.13). Its overseer can be a user, manager or trusted automated system. Graceful interruption, incorporation of new information and frequency controls are explicit questions (§4.2 p.14). IDs can link parent/subagent actions and support targeted response, but IDs can be stolen, leak targeting information or fail to cover agents that use ordinary channels (§3.3 pp.10–11; §4.1 p.13). Useful monitoring therefore needs an explicit observation perimeter, coverage and bypass assumptions.

Certification must stay within what science can establish, connect the claim to the actual instance and permit revocation; context/history changes can stale a claim (§3.2 pp.8–10). Certifiers may be incompetent or captured. The paper's TrustARC example is a warning that certificates can accompany worse trustworthiness, not proof that agent certificates will do so (§3.2 p.9; underlying empirical reference not independently examined here). Likewise identity collection risks privacy, doxxing and chilling whistleblowing (§3.1 pp.7–8). Incident reporting can expose downstream harms unseen by the original deployer, but spurious reports can overwhelm responders (§5.1 pp.17–19). Rollbacks require actual undoing machinery plus a request/execution interface, may be physically impossible, impose uncertainty and costs on counterparties, and create moral hazard (§5.2 pp.19–20). Reverting local state is not sufficient evidence that downstream harm was remedied.

Adoption, interoperability and lock-in are part of I's causal story, not incidental deployment details (§6 pp.20–21). Some controls, including local oversight/rollback, can be useful without widespread adoption, while IDs and communication protocols often depend on counterparties adopting compatible schemes (§2.3 pp.5–6). The paper does **not** choose a universal priority order. Safety and usefulness incentives may diverge from providers' commercial incentives (§4.2 p.14; §4.3 p.15). A locally successful tool does not establish ecosystem coverage.

A proposes programmable cryptography and secure hardware to enable black/white-box access, credible commitments, verifiable forgetting and generative security (pp.6–9). Hardware is currently practical for more applications but offers weaker guarantees; cryptography may impose prohibitive overhead, with stake/overhead determining applicability (pp.6–7). Its v2 research programme comprises formal AI security, cyber-physical security primitives and foundations of generative security (pp.18–20). These are intended research directions, not security proofs supplied by the thesis. Generative security explicitly includes “Succinct and inspectable proofs for correct execution and delegation” (p.20). The report component asks whether agents can prove their chain of interactions; the later auditor component compares protocol specification and implementation (pp.11,17). “Succinct convincing statement” describes an intended interface, not a licence to substitute persuasiveness for evidence.

Its Arena separates downloadable, self-contained **benchmarks** from **challenges** requiring live interaction (p.14). Proposed scoring separates task utility and security-policy compliance, with cost and cross-challenge generality as secondary dimensions (p.15). It proposes theory plus adversarial evaluation rather than assuming that either alone settles all claims (p.13). Appendix II's structured/unstructured examples are expressly exploratory, not commitments (pp.27–28); they include privacy-preserving set intersection, constrained negotiation, physical receipts and disaster response with false reports. Appendix III preserves earlier agentic game-theory ideas while saying this is **not a core pillar** of v2 (pp.28–29). Citing v1's pillars as current would misstate the source.

### Welfare, authority and the cooperation–collusion boundary

All three motivate beneficial interaction, but no one perspective automatically supplies the relevant welfare function. D invokes normative/safety objectives, externality taxes and prevention of power concentration; it recognizes unequal access and capture (§3.1.2 p.8; §§3.4.4,3.4.6 pp.15–16). I explicitly warns that better commitments/cooperation within a coalition can harm actors outside it, including humans less able to use the technology (§4.4 p.17). A foregrounds faithful user representation, privacy and pluralistic infrastructure, while warning that cryptography can enable agents or malicious users to “collude and cartelise against humans” (p.21). It asks who decides society-wide rules (p.22). Thus neither more cooperation nor more legible monitoring is a sufficient outcome metric.

D distinguishes harmful collusion from useful collective capability and notes that tacit algorithmic collusion can arise without direct communication (§3.1.6 p.9). I treats communication as useful for warnings, cooperation and negotiation, while exposing spam, attacks and incentive-driven protocol capture (§4.3 pp.15–16). A lists steganographic concealment as a possible AI advantage (p.8) and later treats collusion as a risk (p.21). Secrecy can serve privacy, and encrypted cooperation is not by itself wrongful. A case must identify the violated policy, affected person or public interest, permitted secrecy, and evidence separating harmful coordination from ordinary cooperation/common incentives.

A material **tension**, rather than a synthesis to silently erase: D says participating components “must all be individually aligned” (§3.2.4 p.12), while A believes coordination can be decoupled from intelligence/alignment enough to make progress with “stupid” and “misaligned” agents (p.7). Their scopes differ: D's complete defence-in-depth market versus A's bounded secure-interaction capability programme. This does not establish an outright logical contradiction, but it rules out treating their shared interest in infrastructure as agreement on minimum trusted components. I offers a complementary framing without claiming infrastructure alone is complete (§8 p.21). Any proposed control argument must name precisely what remains trusted: preferences/policy capture, negotiation authority, runtime/reference monitor, logging, identity/certification, cryptographic assumptions, verifier, and governing institution.

### Source-grounded implications before candidate comparison

The accepted PRD's observation-and-intervention framing is compatible with these sources. The sources broaden what that framing needs to consider: a control decision should state the principals and outsiders, actual authority to alter a collective, information/access per actor, intervention and recovery interfaces, and capability/adoption/incentive assumptions. No accepted scope change follows automatically.

Potential useful questions include (a) when observed compliance supports an intervention or recourse decision given observer coverage and verifier fallibility; (b) when a valid commitment or certified action remains unauthorized by a principal or harmful to outsiders; and (c) which information and control interfaces are required for distributed interruption/recovery when agents create/delegate to other agents. These are source-grounded directions, not claims of novelty, selected cases or an argument to build an infrastructure platform. Each needs a bounded proposition and closest-work examination beyond these broad sources.
